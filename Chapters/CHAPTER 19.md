# Chapter 19 — Organic Reaction Mechanisms from Electronic Structure

*Part II · From Quantum States to Chemical Behavior*

Chapter 18 treated a narrow, highly symmetric class of reactions — pericyclic — where a single preserved symmetry element made the electronic-structure argument almost mechanical. Most of organic chemistry offers no such luxury: an $S_N2$ reaction, an aldol addition, a Friedel–Crafts acylation have no preserved mirror plane or rotation axis to lean on. This chapter asks whether the same underlying tools — frontier orbital interactions (Chapter 17), the Hammond postulate and the distinction between an intermediate and a transition state (Chapter 16), and the Hückel coefficients already computed and checked in Chapter 12 — are enough to derive the named mechanisms of a standard organic chemistry course, or whether each one needs its own separate, unexplained rule.

The opening question:
> **Textbook organic chemistry presents dozens of named mechanisms — $S_N1$, $S_N2$, $E1$, $E2$, electrophilic and nucleophilic aromatic substitution, carbonyl addition, acyl substitution, enolate chemistry, radical chains — largely as a list to memorize. How many genuinely different physical ideas are actually at work underneath that list?**

**The answer, stated before the details.** Remarkably few. Every mechanism in this chapter reduces to some combination of: a two-orbital (occupied–unoccupied) stabilization already derived in Section 17.1; the Hammond postulate of Section 16.13, applied to whichever step is rate-determining; and, in several cases, the *exact* Hückel coefficients of the allyl system already computed in Section 12.5, reused without modification. This chapter's task is not to introduce new physics but to show how far the existing toolkit already reaches.

| Mathematical result                                                     | Chemical destination                                                        |
| ---------------------------------------------------------------------------| ----------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| The two-orbital stabilization of Section 17.1, evaluated at a specific geometry | Backside attack and Walden inversion in $S_N2$                              |
| Hammond's postulate (Section 16.13), applied to an endothermic ionization step | Carbocation-like transition states, and why $S_N1$/$E1$ share one intermediate |
| Sign pattern of $\pi^{*}$ coefficients in a polarized double bond           | Why nucleophiles attack carbonyl carbon, not oxygen, despite oxygen bearing more negative charge |
| The allyl system's nonbonding orbital (Section 12.5), reused with different electron counts | Enolate ambident reactivity (4 electrons) and allylic/benzylic radical stabilization (3 electrons) |
| HOMO-energy and LUMO-energy shifts from a substituent (Section 17.6–17.7) | Directing effects and rate effects in aromatic substitution                 |
| The two-level orbital-mixing formula (Section 11.7), applied to a heteronuclear leaving group | The reactivity order of carboxylic acid derivatives                        |

**Roadmap.** Substitution at saturated carbon (19.1) → elimination (19.2) → substitution on an aromatic ring (19.3) → the carbonyl group, addition and substitution (19.4–19.5) → the enolate, as a direct reuse of Chapter 12's allyl system (19.6) → radicals, the same reuse with one fewer electron (19.7) → what "selectivity" has meant, in total, throughout the chapter (19.8).

## 19.1 Nucleophilic substitution

### $S_N2$

**Backside attack, derived from a two-orbital argument.** A nucleophile's HOMO can stabilize the system by donating into the substrate's lowest unoccupied orbital, the $\sigma^{*}$ antibonding orbital of the C–leaving-group bond (Section 17.1's occupied(Nu)–unoccupied(substrate) pairing, now made geometrically explicit). Like any antibonding combination of two atomic orbitals (Section 10.6), $\sigma^{*}_{\rm C-LG}$ has a node between the carbon and the leaving group, with its two lobes pointing in opposite directions along the C–LG axis — a **larger** lobe on the carbon side (the less electronegative atom typically contributes more to the antibonding combination, Section 19.4 derives this same polarization rule explicitly) and a smaller lobe on the leaving-group side. A nucleophile approaching from the front (the leaving-group side) overlaps with the small lobe and, at short range, is also repelled by the leaving group's own electron density; approaching from directly behind the carbon (**backside attack**) overlaps with the large lobe, maximizing the stabilizing two-orbital interaction with none of the competing repulsion. Backside attack is not an added rule; it is simply where the interaction of Section 17.1 is strongest.

**Walden inversion.** As the nucleophile's bond to carbon strengthens and the C–LG bond weakens along this backside trajectory, the other three substituents on carbon must pass through a planar, $sp^2$-like arrangement at the transition state (Section 16.4's saddle point, with the Nu···C···LG axis as the reaction coordinate) and end up pointing away from the *new* bond exactly as they pointed away from the old one — the geometric consequence of a single, symmetric transition state connecting a tetrahedral reactant to a tetrahedral product with the nucleophile on the opposite face. The stereochemical outcome (inversion at carbon) is therefore a direct consequence of the *same* backside-attack requirement that dictates the reaction's kinetics: both are two faces of one geometric fact.
> **Status of the principle: $S_N2$ backside attack and inversion**
> *Derived*, from the two-orbital stabilization of Section 17.1 combined with the node structure of an antibonding combination (Section 10.6).

### $S_N1$

**A genuine intermediate, and Hammond's postulate applied.** Where the leaving group departs *before* the nucleophile has begun bonding, the rate-determining step is a unimolecular ionization to a planar carbocation — a true intermediate in Section 16.5's sense (a local minimum, however short-lived), stabilized by hyperconjugative donation from adjacent C–H or C–C $\sigma$ bonds into the empty $p$ orbital (the same occupied–unoccupied two-orbital stabilization as Section 17.1, now intramolecular). Because ionization is typically endothermic (breaking a bond heterolytically without yet forming a new one), Section 16.13's Hammond postulate applies directly: the transition state for ionization is **late** and carbocation-like, so whatever stabilizes the carbocation itself (more substituted carbons, adjacent lone pairs, conjugation) also lowers the transition-state energy and accelerates the reaction — the electronic-structure reason tertiary substrates ionize far faster than primary ones.

**Loss of stereochemical information, and rearrangement.** Because the intermediate carbocation is planar, a nucleophile can approach from either face with comparable ease (partial solvent-caging effects aside), giving predominantly racemized product — a direct structural consequence of the intermediate actually existing as a free, symmetric species, unlike the concerted $S_N2$ pathway, which has no such intermediate to lose stereochemical information at. Rearrangements (hydride or alkyl shifts to a neighboring, more stable carbocation) use exactly the same two-orbital hyperconjugative interaction responsible for carbocation stability in the first place: a neighboring $\sigma$ bond that stabilizes the cation by partial donation can, if the resulting shift is favorable enough, complete that donation entirely and migrate.
> **Status of the principle: $S_N1$ mechanism, racemization, and rearrangement**
> *Derived*, from Section 16.5's definition of a genuine intermediate, Section 16.13's Hammond postulate, and the same hyperconjugative two-orbital stabilization used throughout this chapter.

## 19.2 Elimination

### $E2$

**A concerted, geometry-demanding pathway.** As a base removes a $\beta$-hydrogen, the C–H bonding electron pair must simultaneously begin forming the new $\pi$ bond of the alkene, which requires the departing electron pair to end up in a $p$-orbital-like orientation aligned with the emptying $\sigma^{*}_{\rm C-LG}$ orbital exactly as the leaving group departs — the identical requirement, applied to a different bond pair, as Section 19.1's $S_N2$ overlap argument. This alignment is achieved when the C–H and C–LG bonds are **anti-periplanar** (a $180°$ dihedral angle): only in this geometry do the two $p$-orbital-like lobes involved end up parallel and able to develop a continuous, nodeless $\pi$ system as the reaction proceeds, exactly the same phase-continuity requirement already used throughout Chapter 18. A syn-periplanar arrangement is geometrically accessible but gives markedly poorer overlap, and is used only when a rigid ring system makes the anti pathway unavailable.
> **Status of the principle: anti-periplanar $E2$ geometry**
> *Derived*, from the same overlap-continuity requirement used throughout Chapter 18, applied to a concerted proton-loss/bond-formation step rather than a pericyclic ring closure.

### $E1$

**Sharing $S_N1$'s intermediate, and Zaitsev's rule from Hammond's postulate.** $E1$ begins with the identical rate-determining ionization step as $S_N1$ (Section 19.1), the same carbocation intermediate, competing directly for the fate of that one species: nucleophilic capture gives the substitution product, loss of a $\beta$-hydrogen gives the elimination product. Which alkene forms preferentially, when more than one $\beta$-hydrogen is available, follows the same Hammond-postulate logic as before: deprotonation to form a $\pi$ bond is itself a step whose transition state resembles the alkene product forming, so whichever alkene is more stable (more substituted, more hyperconjugatively stabilized — the same stabilization already invoked for the carbocation itself) has the lower-energy transition state and forms faster, in addition to being the thermodynamically favored product once equilibrated — **Zaitsev's rule**, not an independent empirical regularity but the same stability argument used twice, once for kinetics (via Hammond) and once for thermodynamics (Chapter 15).
> **Status of the principle: the shared $S_N1/E1$ intermediate and Zaitsev's rule**
> *Derived*, from Section 16.5's intermediate concept and Section 16.13's Hammond postulate, applied to the same carbocation already introduced in Section 19.1.

## 19.3 Aromatic substitution

### Electrophilic aromatic substitution

**The arene as nucleophile.** An aromatic ring's filled $\pi$ system serves as the HOMO donor (Section 17.6's nucleophile role, here played by the whole ring rather than a lone pair), attacking an electrophile's LUMO to form a non-aromatic, resonance-stabilized cyclohexadienyl cation — the **arenium ion** (or Wheland intermediate), a genuine intermediate in Section 16.5's sense, with the positive charge delocalized over three ring positions by exactly the kind of resonance already made quantitative for the allyl cation in Section 12.5. Loss of a proton restores the aromatic system, recovering the substantial delocalization stabilization already quantified in Section 12.4 — the strong thermodynamic driving force for rearomatization.

**Directing effects, derived from where the resonance structures put the charge.** An electron-donating substituent (an amine or ether oxygen's lone pair, for instance) raises the ring's HOMO energy (Section 17.6), narrowing the gap to the electrophile's LUMO and accelerating attack everywhere on the ring (Section 17.4's stabilization grows as the denominator shrinks) — but the *arenium ion's* resonance structures place positive charge specifically on the carbons ortho and para to the point of attack, and only when attack occurs ortho or para to the donor substituent can that substituent's lone pair directly stabilize one of those resonance structures by additional donation. Attack at the meta position never places positive charge adjacent to the donor, forfeiting this extra stabilization. **The same substituent, through the same electronic mechanism (raising a frontier orbital energy and, through the intermediate's resonance structures, selectively stabilizing specific ring positions), explains both the rate enhancement and the ortho/para regiochemistry** — two textbook "rules" reduced to one mechanism. An electron-withdrawing substituent reverses both effects by the identical logic run in reverse: it lowers the ring HOMO (deactivating, slower reaction overall) and destabilizes ortho/para attack specifically (since those resonance structures would place positive charge directly on or adjacent to an already electron-poor position), leaving meta as the least-disfavored site.

### Nucleophilic aromatic substitution

**The arene as electrophile.** With one or more strong electron-withdrawing groups lowering the ring's LUMO sufficiently (Section 17.7's electrophile logic, now applied to the whole ring), a nucleophile can instead attack the ring directly, forming an anionic **Meisenheimer complex** — the electronic mirror image of the arenium ion, stabilized by resonance structures that place negative charge specifically ortho and para to the electron-withdrawing groups, exactly the reverse of the directing argument above. Loss of the leaving group restores aromaticity. This addition–elimination pathway requires strong activation (multiple electron-withdrawing groups) precisely because it is the opposite electronic demand from electrophilic aromatic substitution: a substituent that accelerates one pathway (by raising the ring HOMO) necessarily decelerates the other (by also raising the ring LUMO, Section 17.5's general point that a single substituent shifts both frontier orbitals, usually in the same direction).

**The benzyne mechanism, and orbital orthogonality.** When no adequate Meisenheimer pathway is available but a strong base and a poor leaving-group arrangement are present, base removal of an *ortho*-hydrogen concerted with loss of the leaving group generates a highly strained **aryne** (benzyne) intermediate: an extra, in-plane $\pi$-type bond formed from two $sp^2$ orbitals pointing toward each other across the ring, geometrically forced to lie in the plane of the ring rather than perpendicular to it. Because this in-plane bond is orthogonal, by symmetry, to the ring's ordinary $\pi$ system (Section 11.7's vanishing theorem again: two orbitals related by a symmetry operation that maps one to minus itself cannot mix with orbitals that are symmetric under the same operation), it does not participate in or disrupt the ring's aromaticity, but it is a poor, strained overlap far from the ordinary triple-bond geometry, making benzyne highly reactive. A nucleophile can add to either carbon of this strained bond with little geometric preference, explaining the loss of strict regiochemical fidelity (attack at either the original or the adjacent ring position) characteristic of benzyne-mediated substitutions.
> **Status of the principle: aromatic substitution mechanisms**
> *Derived*, from the same frontier-orbital-energy arguments (Sections 17.6–17.7) and resonance/intermediate concepts (Section 16.5, Section 12.5) used throughout this chapter, with the benzyne case additionally relying on the orbital-orthogonality argument of Section 11.7.

## 19.4 Carbonyl chemistry

**Where the $\pi^{*}$ coefficient is largest, and why.** Applying the two-level mixing formula already used throughout this book (Section 11.7, Section 12.2) to the carbon–oxygen $\pi$ bond: oxygen's $2p$ orbital lies lower in energy than carbon's (oxygen is more electronegative — a statement now traceable, via Section 17.8, to a specific difference in $I$ and $A$), so, exactly as in any heteronuclear two-level mixing problem, the lower-energy atomic orbital (oxygen's) contributes *more* to the resulting bonding combination, and the higher-energy atomic orbital (carbon's) contributes *more* to the antibonding combination. **The carbonyl $\pi^{*}$ (the LUMO) therefore has its largest coefficient on carbon**, not oxygen — even though oxygen, as the more electronegative atom, carries the larger share of *negative charge* in the bonding $\pi$ orbital. This is a clean, concrete instance of Section 17.5's charge-control/orbital-control distinction: a purely electrostatic argument might suggest a nucleophile is drawn toward the positively-charged carbon anyway (charge control), while the orbital argument independently predicts attack at carbon because that is where the LUMO coefficient — and hence the two-orbital stabilization of Section 17.1 — is largest (orbital control). **Both arguments agree here**, which is part of why nucleophilic addition to carbonyl carbon is such a reliable, general reaction; Section 19.6 shows a case (the enolate) where the two arguments instead disagree and the outcome depends on which nucleophile is used.

**The Bürgi–Dunitz trajectory.** Nucleophiles do not approach the carbonyl carbon along the C=O axis, but at an angle of about $105$–$107°$ from it, a value originally established empirically from a statistical survey of crystal structures containing a nucleophilic atom near a carbonyl group, and separately confirmed by direct calculation on the hydride–formaldehyde system. Frontier-orbital reasoning rationalizes the qualitative shape of this result: approach directly along the C=O axis ($180°$) would align the incoming lone pair with the *node* of the $\pi^{*}$ orbital, giving no net overlap at all, while an obtuse angle allows the nucleophile's orbital to overlap constructively with the larger, carbon-centered lobe of $\pi^{*}$ (Section 19.4's polarization result) while tilting away from the oxygen's developing negative charge and lone pairs. **The precise numerical value, however, is not something this qualitative argument derives**; it is an empirical, crystallographically established number that a two-orbital picture explains only in direction, not in degree, and more recent quantitative work shows that Pauli repulsion and direct electrostatics contribute alongside the orbital-overlap effect emphasized here.
> **Status of the principle: carbonyl LUMO polarization and the Bürgi–Dunitz trajectory**
> *Derived* for the polarization of $\pi^{*}$ toward carbon, from the two-level mixing argument of Section 11.7; the qualitative obtuseness of the approach angle is *Derived* from the same overlap argument, but the specific numerical value of the Bürgi–Dunitz angle is *Empirical*, established crystallographically and only partially rationalized (not derived in full) by frontier-orbital reasoning alone.

## 19.5 Acyl substitution

**What makes an acyl compound different from a simple carbonyl.** A ketone or aldehyde has no group on the carbonyl carbon capable of leaving as a stable anion, so nucleophilic addition (Section 19.4) is the final step: the tetrahedral alkoxide intermediate simply protonates. An acyl compound (an acid chloride, anhydride, ester, or amide) carries a substituent — Cl, OR, or NR$_2$ — that *can* leave, so the same initial nucleophilic addition (via the identical Bürgi–Dunitz trajectory) produces a tetrahedral intermediate (Section 16.5's genuine local minimum) that can collapse a second way: by expelling that substituent and reforming the C=O bond, giving net **substitution** rather than simple addition.

**The reactivity order, derived from one two-orbital argument used twice.** Acid chlorides react fastest, followed by anhydrides, then esters, then amides (essentially unreactive under ordinary conditions) — a well-known ordering, and a derivable one. The leaving group's lone pair (on Cl, O, or N) can donate into the same carbonyl $\pi^{*}$ orbital just discussed (Section 19.4), by the identical two-level mixing logic: this donation *raises* the $\pi^{*}$ (LUMO) energy (an occupied–unoccupied mixing that pushes the unoccupied level up, Section 11.7's two-level formula run for the *upper* level this time) and correspondingly *reduces* the carbonyl's electrophilicity (Section 17.7). Nitrogen's lone pair, closest in energy to the carbonyl $\pi$ system and with the best geometric overlap, donates most strongly — most strongly deactivating an amide's carbonyl, and simultaneously making amide nitrogen an exceptionally poor leaving group (a stable, high-lying, effectively non-basic-anion-averse nitrogen lone pair does not want to leave as a leaving group either). Chlorine's lone pair is lower in energy and more diffuse, overlapping poorly with the carbonyl $\pi$ system (a large energy denominator in Section 11.7's mixing formula, hence weak donation): the carbonyl stays strongly electrophilic, *and* chloride, precisely because it does not donate well into the ring in the first place, is simultaneously an excellent, stable leaving group. **One physical effect — how well the substituent's lone pair mixes with the carbonyl $\pi$ system — governs both halves of the reactivity order at once.**
> **Status of the principle: the acyl-substitution reactivity order**
> *Derived*, from the same two-level orbital-mixing argument (Section 11.7) used throughout the chapter, applied to the substituent lone pair rather than to the incoming nucleophile.

## 19.6 Enolate chemistry

**An exact reuse of the allyl system.** Deprotonating the carbon adjacent to a carbonyl group produces a carbanion immediately stabilized by delocalization into the carbonyl $\pi^{*}$ — an O–C–C array of three $p$ orbitals carrying four electrons, **isoelectronic in its $\pi$ framework with the allyl anion already solved exactly in Section 12.5.** No new calculation is needed: the same Hückel coefficients apply directly, with the nonbonding orbital (now the enolate's HOMO, holding the extra electron pair) having **zero coefficient at the central atom** — here, the original carbonyl carbon — and equal, nonzero coefficients at the two termini — here, the oxygen and the $\alpha$-carbon.

**Ambident reactivity, derived rather than merely observed.** This is precisely the situation already introduced, in the abstract, as the motivating example for Section 17.13's dual descriptor: a species reactive at more than one site. The enolate's HOMO places electron density equally at oxygen and at the $\alpha$-carbon (Section 12.5's coefficients, reused without modification) and none at all at the central carbon — so an electrophile can bond at either terminus (**O-alkylation** or **C-alkylation**), and Section 17.14's HSAB principle now decides which: a hard, small, charge-localized electrophile (a proton, a hard metal-bound alkylating agent) prefers the harder, more electronegative, more charge-concentrated oxygen terminus (charge control, Section 17.5); a soft, large, polarizable electrophile (a soft alkyl halide under $S_N2$-like conditions, Section 19.1) prefers the softer, more polarizable carbon terminus (orbital control). **This is Chapter 17's HSAB principle and Chapter 12's allyl-system mathematics, combined without modification, giving a complete and testable account of enolate regiochemistry that neither chapter alone could provide.**
> **Status of the principle: enolate ambident reactivity**
> *Derived*, as a direct, unmodified reapplication of Section 12.5's allyl-anion coefficients (isoelectronic reuse) combined with Section 17.14's HSAB principle.

## 19.7 Radical chemistry

**One fewer electron in a familiar orbital array.** A radical carries one unpaired electron in a singly occupied molecular orbital (SOMO), generated most commonly by **homolytic** bond cleavage (each fragment keeping one electron, in contrast to the heterolytic cleavage underlying $S_N1$/$E1$'s ionic intermediates, Section 19.1) under thermal or photochemical initiation. Radical reactions typically proceed as **chains**: initiation (homolysis), **propagation** steps in which a radical abstracts an atom or adds across a $\pi$ bond while regenerating a new radical (net charge-neutral throughout, unlike every ionic mechanism earlier in this chapter), and termination (two radicals combining).

**Allylic and benzylic stabilization, the same allyl system a third time.** An allylic radical's three-$p$-orbital, $\pi$-electron array is, once again, exactly Section 12.5's allyl system — this time with **three** electrons rather than the enolate's four or the allyl cation's two, occupying the bonding orbital fully and the nonbonding orbital singly. The unpaired electron therefore sits in the identical nonbonding orbital already computed, with equal density at both termini and none at the center: an allylic radical is stabilized (relative to a hypothetical localized radical) by exactly the same delocalization energy already computed for the allyl cation and anion in Section 12.5, and is reactive (in subsequent propagation or combination steps) at either terminus, for the identical structural reason as the enolate's ambident reactivity. Benzylic radical stability follows the same logic, delocalizing into the larger $\pi$ system of an attached aromatic ring.

**A closed loop back to Chapter 13.** Because radicals carry a net electron spin, they are the direct subject of the EPR spectroscopy already derived in Section 13.13: the hyperfine splitting pattern observed for an allylic or benzylic radical is a direct experimental readout of exactly the delocalized SOMO density this section has just derived from Chapter 12's coefficients, closing a loop between a spectroscopic technique developed six chapters ago and the reactivity picture developed here.
> **Status of the principle: allylic and benzylic radical stabilization**
> *Derived*, as a third, unmodified reuse of Section 12.5's allyl-system coefficients, this time at three-electron (radical) occupation.

## 19.8 Reaction selectivity

**What this chapter has actually assembled.** Every named mechanism in this chapter — $S_N1$, $S_N2$, $E1$, $E2$, electrophilic and nucleophilic aromatic substitution, carbonyl addition, acyl substitution, enolate alkylation, radical propagation — has been derived from a small, fixed set of tools built in earlier chapters: the two-orbital stabilization of Section 17.1, the Hammond postulate of Section 16.13, the intermediate-versus-transition-state distinction of Section 16.4–16.5, and, remarkably, the *exact same* allyl-system coefficients from Section 12.5, reused without any recalculation across three entirely different chemical contexts (enolates, allylic radicals, and — via the arenium and Meisenheimer intermediates of Section 19.3 — cationic and anionic aromatic intermediates as well).

**Chemoselectivity, regioselectivity, and stereoselectivity are not three separate phenomena requiring three separate explanations.** A reaction's *chemoselectivity* (which functional group reacts) traces to relative frontier-orbital gaps (Section 17.4–17.5); its *regioselectivity* (which position on a given group reacts) traces to frontier-orbital coefficients at specific atoms (Section 17.12's Fukui functions, used explicitly in Sections 19.3 and 19.6); its *stereoselectivity* (which three-dimensional outcome results) traces to the specific overlap geometry required for maximal two-orbital stabilization (Sections 19.1–19.2's backside attack and anti-periplanar elimination). **No reaction in this chapter "just happens" to be selective; every case of selectivity examined here is traceable to a specific, already-derived orbital-energy or orbital-overlap argument**, in keeping with the philosophy stated at the very start of this book: there are no arbitrary exceptions, only approximations — and, as this chapter has shown at length, often the very same approximation, applied again.
> **Status of the principle: reaction selectivity as a unified consequence of frontier-orbital theory**
> *Derived* — a summary statement about the structure of the arguments made throughout this chapter, not a new physical claim in itself.
